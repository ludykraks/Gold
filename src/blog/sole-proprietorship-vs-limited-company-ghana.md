A sole proprietorship is important if you are the only owner and want a straightforward business structure.This is what people usually term one-man business.

A private company limited by shares deserves a closer look if you want shared ownership or legal separation between yourself and the business.

This simple-to-understand guide compares a sole proprietorship with a **private company limited by shares**.


## What Is a Sole Proprietorship?

A sole proprietorship is a business owned by one person. The Office of the Registrar of Companies (ORC) describes its business-name registration service this way and states that the owner has unlimited liability. Registering the name does not protect your personal assets from business liabilities. This means that if the business is in debt or faces liquidy, the owner's personal assets can be used to settle the debts.

Before choosing this structure, think about what would happen if the business could not pay a supplier or meet a contractual obligation.

## What Is a Private Company Limited by Shares?

An incorporated company has its own legal identity. Its shareholders' liability as members is limited to any unpaid amount on their shares. Ghana's Companies Act permits one or more people to form a company, so a solo founder can consider this option too.

However, one shareholder does not mean one director. Section 171 requires at least two directors, with at least one ordinarily resident in Ghana. Ownership and management are different roles. 

Do not treat limited liability as permission to ignore risk. 

## The Main Differences at a Glance

| Question | Sole proprietorship | Private company limited by shares |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who owns it? | One proprietor | One or more shareholders |
| Is there a separate legal identity? | No | Yes |
| What is the basic liability position? | The proprietor has unlimited liability | Shareholders' liability as members is generally limited to unpaid amounts on their shares |
| What recurring ORC filing should you plan for? | Annual business-name renewal | Company annual returns |


## Which Structure Fits Your Plans?

Use these examples as questions to guide you:

### A solo designer taking small projects

Imagine a graphic designer who works alone and takes on a few projects each month. A sole proprietorship could be an option to discuss if the designer values straightforward administration.

The useful question is not just “Do I work alone?” It is “What could I owe if a project goes wrong, and am I comfortable accepting that exposure?”

### Two founders building an online store

Imagine two friends in Accra planning to contribute money, buy stock and run an ecommerce business together. A company is worth discussing with a professional because they want a shared-ownership arrangement.

Before registering anything, they should agree how much each person contributes, how decisions will be made and what happens if one wants to leave. A certificate is not a substitute for that conversation.

## Compare Ongoing Work, Not Just Registration Fees

The ORC requires annual renewal of a registered sole-proprietor business name. Its company annual-return guidance lists financial statements and an auditor's report among filing requirements. These are different obligations, not two names for the same payment.


## Does a Limited Company Automatically Pay Less Tax?

Do not assume that it does.

The Ghana Revenue Authority includes sole proprietors within its [personal income tax guidance](https://gra.gov.gh/domestic-tax/personal-income-tax/). Companies fall within the [corporate income tax framework](https://gra.gov.gh/domestic-tax/tax-types/corporate-income-tax/), where the applicable rate can depend on the activity, industry and location.

Ask an accountant to compare both structures using your expected income, expenses and how you plan to take money out of the business. Comparing a company tax rate with an individual's rate alone is not enough to make the decision.

## Five Questions to Answer Before Registering

1. Will I be the only owner, or will someone else have an ownership stake?
2. What debts, contracts or customer claims could the business face?
3. Might I seek investment or bring in another owner?
4. Who will handle records, filings and professional appointments?

You will get more useful guidance from a clear description of your business than from asking which structure is “best” in general.
